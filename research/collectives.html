Abstract

Groups of language-model agents now debate, verify and divide work, yet how they are wired, supervised and rewarded is mostly set by hand. We model an agent collective as a complex dynamical network: each agent’s answer is a node state, messages are diffusive coupling through the communication graph, and verifiers are pinning control inputs. Building on pinning control of nonlinear complex networks [Ghaffari & Arebi, 2016], we derive three results.

First, rounds to consensus are governed by the algebraic connectivity λ2, the best achievable speed by the eigenratio λN/λ2, and over-agreeable agents oscillate once their coupling exceeds 2/λN. Second, a Lyapunov criterion gives the minimum coupling for convergence to the verified answer and a lower bound on the number of verifiers, k > αN/g. Third, a potential-based group reward for reinforcement learning pays each round’s Lyapunov decrease toward the verified solution and charges an LQR-style cost for communication.

In simulation with 64 agents, the spectral prediction ranks six topologies almost exactly as observed (Spearman 0.94), Theorem 1 matches the simulated minimum coupling to within a median 10%, and no group with fewer verifiers than the bound converges. Four small pilots with groups of eight Claude Haiku 5.5 agents (one model, 30 to 60 problems per condition) give preliminary evidence consistent with both regimes of the theory. On GSM8K every agent is right on its own, errors decay without control, and a persuasive adversary moves no one. On AIME competition problems, where single agents are right 72 to 80% of the time, debate alone gains 10 to 17 points on every one of five topologies, and the two graphs with the lowest algebraic connectivity, the star and the scale-free graph, reach less of that gain after the first exchange (80% against 95%), in the direction the spectral analysis predicts.

In a controlled pilot, verifying the two highest-degree agents made the group faster (4 points more accuracy after the first exchange, 95% CI 0.1 to 7.6) and about 15% cheaper per correct answer, and a stubborn adversary at the hub captured 0.2% of honest agents instead of 1.2%. The lift in final accuracy is small (2 to 4 points), because debate alone already reaches 0.93, and at eight agents where the verifiers sit made no measurable difference. Stated confidence is calibrated and separates agents that will end right from those that will end wrong from the first round, which makes it a nearly free state estimate for confidence-weighted coupling. A sparse scale-free network matched a fully connected one on accuracy with 12 links instead of 28, reading 28% fewer input tokens. These pilots are preliminary; the larger experiments that would test the theory at scale are specified but not yet run.
